# Borrow the feed, refuse the dark patterns

Research behind `docs/LAUNCH.md` (Oct 28, 2026): TikTok-style and microlearning apps, plus App Store rules for late 2026. Every figure links to its source.

The apps that do vertical-feed learning well take TikTok's gestures, which users already know, and Duolingo's forgiving habit loops. They show a clear, honest paywall in the first session. Most of what users find confusing or hostile is the other side of that coin: no cue to swipe, gesture meanings that clash, stale "continue" states, paywalls before any value, overlapping subscription listings, and guilt-framed copy. The money case for the onboarding paywall is strong. **53% of subscription revenue starts in the first session**, and onboarding paywalls earn about **$170 per 1,000 views against $40 later** ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/state-of-paywalls/report/)). The best-documented trust fix is just as strong: Blinkist's transparent trial timeline cut complaints by **55%** and *raised* trial sign-ups by **23%** ([UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/how-solving-our-biggest-customer-complaint-at-blinkist-led-to-a-23-increase-in-conversion-b60ad514134b)). Streaks work best when the rule is easy and forgiving. Duolingo found a 7-day streak is the "magic threshold" ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)). Shipping in late 2026 means building with Xcode 26 and the iOS 26 SDK, meeting strict subscription disclosure rules under guideline 3.1.2, adding a "check with a doctor" reminder under 1.4.1 for medical content, and opening Amazon links outside the app. For Scroll Ready, the path is a play-first guest feed, a soft paywall placed after the user gets value, one calm daily reminder, and a short tab bar where each tab has one job. The evidence is uneven. The Duolingo and paywall-benchmark data are solid. Much of the gesture and layout guidance rests on design critiques and third-party teardowns rather than controlled studies.

## Familiar gestures carry the feed, but a niche app must teach them

Short-video apps share a layout grammar that users now expect. Captions, username, and sound sit in a bottom overlay, and a vertical action rail (follow, like, comment, share) runs down the right edge. Third-party creator guides tell creators to keep content out of roughly the **top 220 px and bottom 450–480 px of a 1080×1920 frame** on TikTok and Reels ([WildAndFree Tools](https://wildandfreetools.com/blog/vertical-video-safe-zones-guide-2026/)). These numbers come from a vendor blog, not official platform documentation, so treat them as approximate. A design critique of TikTok finds the rail's heart and comment icons work because users know them from other apps. The share arrow works better because it opens a sheet of *labeled* options. The same critique lists real flaws. TikTok gives **no cue to swipe up after first login** and relies on users' "knowledge of the world." A paused video is ambiguous unless a play glyph appears. Long captions cover the content. The right-side rail is awkward for left-handed users ([Pratt IXD](https://ixd.prattsi.org/2022/01/design-critique-tiktok-ios-app-2/)). TikTok can skip a swipe tutorial because of its scale. A new niche app can't.

TikTok also offers the closest precedent for a text-heavy learning card. **Photo Mode carousels** nest *horizontal* swipes between 2–35 slides inside the vertical feed, auto-advance after about 5 seconds, and follow the rule "one idea per slide." If a viewer can't get the point in a half-second glance, the slide is too dense ([Contentdrips](https://contentdrips.com/blog/2026/08/how-to-post-a-carousel-on-tiktok-step-by-step-2026/)). The risk for any feed that mixes in multi-step cards is that the gestures clash. Stories-style segment bars teach tap-right/tap-left and auto-advance, carousels teach horizontal swipes, and the feed teaches vertical swipes. No usability study was found on segment bars inside a vertical feed, so this is the biggest open question to test with users. The safest reading of the precedents is this: if segments appear, they should respond to taps, and for reading content they should fill only when a step is finished, not on a timer. Imprint's reviews show a related failure. A finished course "keeps appearing in my home for me to 'continue'" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/id1482780647)).

Inline quizzes follow Duolingo's well-documented pattern. The Check/Continue button stays disabled until the user picks an answer. A correct answer brings short praise, and a wrong answer reveals the correct one with a "GOT IT" button ([Screensdesign](https://screensdesign.com/apps/duolingo-language-lessons/)). Duolingo can block progress because its lessons are linear. No source shows a scrolling feed that blocks swiping until a quiz is answered. For safety content, "GOT IT" with only the correct answer is too thin; a wrong answer should also explain *why*. On readability, Apple sets the iOS default text size at **17 pt and the minimum at 11 pt**. Body text scales up to **47 pt at the largest accessibility size**, and Apple recommends built-in text styles so Dynamic Type and Bold Text work ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography.md)). A fixed full-screen card that looks clean at 17 pt will overflow at 40 pt. Cards need a fallback that scrolls inside the card and never cuts off text.

Scroll The Bible is the model Scroll Ready names, and its structure is simple. The free layer is "one smooth vertical scroll." Tapping a verse opens labeled study sections (Deep Study, Original Language, Explain It Easier, Related Verses), and those sections are the premium layer ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/ca/app/id6756558351)). Its weak point is trust. Its privacy policy says the deeper explanations are OpenAI-generated and "can be inaccurate or incomplete," and an independent reviewer flags overlapping subscription listings as confusing ([Learn of Christ](https://learnofchrist.com/resources/scroll-the-bible)). Its ratings data conflict between storefronts: 4.9 from 146 ratings in Canada, and 4.8 from 18K on the main listing.

## The first-session paywall pays, but honesty decides whether it hurts

Paid-first learning apps (Headway, Imprint, Calm, Scroll The Bible) follow nearly the same script. They open with a value carousel, run a goal or interest quiz, show a "building your plan" loader, sometimes add a commitment ritual, and end on a trial-timeline paywall shown before the first real content. Across 40 onboarding flows, the **median first paywall sits at step 11 of about 17** ([Lazyweb](https://www.lazyweb.com/research/how-many-steps-before-the-paywall-in-onboarding)). Headway goes furthest. It asks for sign-up before anything else, runs an **~18-second "Crafting Learning Experience" loader**, has users "seal" a commitment with a fingerprint, and follows with lifetime-deal upsells ([Screensdesign](https://screensdesign.com/apps/headway-daily-micro-learning/)). Content-first apps take the opposite approach. Duolingo opens with "Just 7 quick questions before we start your first lesson!" and holds monetization until after the first lesson ([Screensdesign](https://screensdesign.com/articles/duolingo-onboarding-design/)). Moving sign-up later famously produced **about a 20% increase in DAUs** ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker)). Scroll The Bible's paywall headline, "Get Addicted to the Bible Instead," and its screen-time guilt opener show that conversion copy can work in a faith app with a high rating ([Screensdesign](https://screensdesign.com/apps/scroll-the-bible/)). That framing is exactly what a calm brand should not copy. Calm shows the alternative: its first screen simply says "take a deep breath," and it presents results as "recommended for you based on your responses" ([Appcues](https://goodux.appcues.com/blog/calm-app-new-user-experience)).

The benchmark data explain why nearly everyone puts the paywall in onboarding, and they also show the cost.

Hard paywalls win on raw conversion. Soft paywalls convert about **50% better** in Education, while hard paywalls earn **21% more LTV per subscriber** ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/blog/markdown/education-app-subscription-benchmarks.md)). Pre-selecting annual doesn't hurt conversion, and 22% of apps delay the close button by 3–5 seconds ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/state-of-paywalls/report/)). That delay is common, but nothing requires it. The complaints show where this goes wrong. Scroll The Bible's 1-star reviews describe forgetting a trial, a denied refund, and a missing cancel link ("THEY NEVER SENT THE LINK TO MY EMAIL!"). Another reviewer praises the fact that "you don't need a subscription to use this app after the trial runs out" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6756558351)). Blinkist fixed the same complaint by promising a reminder **two days before the trial ended**. Notification opt-in went from **6% to 74%**, complaints dropped 55%, and trial sign-ups rose 23% ([B2B Pricing Insights](https://b2bpricinginsights.substack.com/p/4-min-read-how-blinkists-new-paywall)). That is the strongest single result in this research: honesty raised conversion. Blinkist also later sent discount offers of up to 75% off, which a calm brand may not want to copy.

Notification permission follows the same logic. iOS opt-in averages about **44–49%** ([MobiLoud](https://www.mobiloud.com/blog/push-notification-opt-in-rate)). The system prompt can be shown only once, so apps first show their own screen explaining the benefit. Headway ties the ask to a reminder time the user picked. Scroll The Bible asks after purchase. Blinkist was criticized for asking at 23 seconds in, before showing any value ([Screensdesign](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/blinkist-book-summaries-daily)). On accounts, Apple's guideline 5.1.1(v) says apps without significant account-based features must let people use them without a login. If any third-party login is offered, guideline 4.8 effectively requires Sign in with Apple ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)).

## Habit loops work when they forgive, and when they stay simple

Duolingo has the best published evidence on streaks. After **more than 600 streak experiments**, it found retention jumps at each early streak length up to day 7. Users who opted in to a streak goal on purpose engaged more. And "the more that we can make the feature easily comprehensible to users, the more retentive it is" ([Lenny Distilled](https://lennydistilled.pages.dev/episodes/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)). Letting a single lesson extend the streak, separate from the daily goal, raised Day-14 retention by **3.3%** and increased learners on a 7+ day streak by **over 40%**. Users with a 7-day streak were **2.4x more likely to return the next day** ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)). Streak Freezes and milestone animations added smaller gains, and Duolingo cites research that giving people slack toward goals motivates more than rigid rules ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)). About half of Education and Health & Fitness apps have a streak screen. Lazyweb advises adding one only for "a daily-repeat behavior worth reinforcing" ([Lazyweb](https://www.lazyweb.com/research/how-common-are-streak-screens-outside-duolingo.md)). The backlash is real but only lightly evidenced, coming from interviews and social posts. Losing a long streak gives loyal users "a convenient exit point," attention shifts to the count instead of the learning, and permanent "lost streak" badges remind users of failure. The proposed fixes are weekly goals, a "take a break" option, and celebrating what was learned when a streak ends ([UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/3-reframing-streaks-on-duolingo-5-ideas-for-a-more-healthy-and-flexible-approach-to-language-8fd89545771e)). Calm sets a gentle example: users can repair a broken streak by logging a missed session ([Calm](https://support.calm.com/hc/en-us/articles/360008704893)). Exit modals that warn about lost XP (Duolingo's "Keep Learning / Quit") suit gamified apps but lean toward fear.

Daily reminders work best when they are tied to the user's own routine and the copy rotates. Duolingo sends its first reminder **about 23.5 hours after the previous practice** ([Lenny Distilled](https://lennydistilled.pages.dev/episodes/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)). A bandit algorithm picks the copy and demotes recently seen templates, because new messages persuade at first and then stop working ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/hi-its-duo-the-ai-behind-the-meme/)). Industry surveys say people opt out because there are **too many messages (51%)** or they are **irrelevant (40%)**. Users who got at least one push in their first 90 days had about one-third higher retention ([MobiLoud](https://www.mobiloud.com/blog/push-notification-statistics)); this is a secondary compilation. Widgets are an underused anchor. Half of Duolingo learners with its widget have a 6+ month streak. The widget shows only the streak and whether it's at risk, because extra stats weren't needed ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/widget-feature)). Scroll The Bible and YouVersion build habits around verse-of-the-day widgets and seasonal peaks. YouVersion saw record engagement on Easter, with about 19M people ([Christian Post](https://assets.christianpost.com/news/youversion-reveals-top-bible-verse-of-2025.html)).

In navigation, the common complaint is overlap, not a lack of features. Headway's feed and Library are criticized because their roles "could be clearer," and its Library sub-tabs could be streamlined ([Screensdesign](https://screensdesign.com/showcase/headway-daily-micro-learning)). Baymard's large-scale testing found users overwhelmed beyond **about 10 options per level**. It recommends clickable parent categories with thumbnails and warns that very thin categories are dead ends ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-navigation-best-practice)). No study looked at "coming soon" tiles or composite "readiness scores" directly. The nearest evidence points to progress that users can understand at a glance and that never drops, because losses are remembered more than gains.

## Shipping to the App Store in late 2026 is mostly a disclosure exercise

Apple's guidelines were last updated **October 16, 2026** ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). About **23% of 2025 submissions were rejected**, and performance and completeness issues appear in roughly 65% of rejections ([Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/app-review-what-reviewers-look-for-2026), citing Apple's transparency report). Apple says 90% of reviews finish within 24 hours, but forum threads from 2026 report waits of two weeks or more ([Apple forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/841016)). The rules that matter most for a subscription survival-skills feed built with Expo are below.

| Area | Requirement | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Build toolchain | Xcode 26 / iOS 26 SDK since Apr 28, 2026; target iOS 13+ since Sep 9, 2026 | [Apple](https://www.developer.apple.com/news/upcoming-requirements/) |
| Completeness (2.1) | No placeholders, all URLs working, IAP visible to the reviewer; over 40% of unresolved issues are 2.1 | [Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/), [RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/the-ultimate-guide-to-app-store-rejections.md) |
| Subscriptions (3.1.2) | Billed amount and period shown prominently, trial terms, cancellation language, Restore, Terms (EULA) link in the binary *and* the description | [RevenueCat docs](https://www.revenuecat.com/docs/tools/paywalls/creating-paywalls/app-review), [RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/the-ultimate-guide-to-app-store-rejections.md) |
| Medical content (1.4.1) | Greater scrutiny; "remind users to check with a doctor" | [Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/) |
| Privacy (5.1.1/5.1.2) | Policy link in metadata and in the app; consent even for "anonymous" usage data; ATT only if tracking | [Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/) |

| OTA updates | Fixes and content are fine; core-purpose or payment-flow changes are not (2.5.2, DPLA 3.3) | [Bitrise](https://bitrise.io/blog/post/what-app-stores-allow-with-ota-updates-apple-and-google-policy-explained) |
| iPad | iPhone-only apps must still "behave properly when running on iPad" | [Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/app-review-what-reviewers-look-for-2026) |
| Amazon affiliate links | Must open in the Amazon app or the native browser, never an in-app web view | [Amazon Associates](https://affiliate-program.amazon.com/help/node/topic/GW7ZSEASVXHFPZCY) |
| Rating prompts | Apple's API only; at most 3 times a year; never triggered by a button tap | [Guidelines 5.6.1](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/), [Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/storekit/skstorereviewcontroller/requestreview(in:).md) |

Four things affect Scroll Ready specifically. First, guideline 4.2 sends "simply a book" apps to Apple Books and rejects apps that are mainly "a collection of links." The app must come across as an interactive learning feed, and gear links must not dominate it ([Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Second, a secondary summary of Amazon's policy says affiliate apps must be free to download with free link access ([ConductAtlas](https://conductatlas.com/platform/amazon-associates/amazon-associates-program-policies/provision/CA-P-064535/mobile-app-must-be-free-to-download-with-free-link-access/)). Gear links should therefore stay outside the paywall. Whether SFSafariViewController counts as a "web view" isn't stated, so opening links in the external browser is the safe choice. Physical goods fall outside the in-app purchase rules. Third, with Expo, use `runtimeVersion: { policy: "fingerprint" }` so a JavaScript update can't land on an incompatible build ([Expo](https://docs.expo.dev/eas-update/runtime-versions/)). Ship paywall changes only as store builds, and send card content through EAS Update. Liquid Glass icons go in `ios.icon` (supported since SDK 54), but Icon Composer runs only on macOS ([Expo](https://expo.dev/changelog/sdk-54)). Fourth, Apple's **Accessibility Nutrition Labels** are voluntary for now but will become required. A feature counts only if users can complete every common task with it, including first launch and purchase ([App Store Connect Help](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-accessibility/overview-of-accessibility-nutrition-labels)). A swipe-only feed can't claim VoiceOver support without accessible next and previous actions. Enrollment costs **$99 per year**. An organization account needs a D-U-N-S number and a working website, while an individual account shows the person's legal name as the seller ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/programs/enroll/)). One gap remains: no authoritative source says whether reviewers treat wild-food or survival content under 1.4. Assume the stricter reading.

## What this means for Scroll Ready

**Feed and cards.** Use the TikTok grammar but teach it once. Add a gentle swipe hint on the first card, keep card text clear of the bottom ~25% and the right rail, and give Save a text label or a sheet with labels rather than a bare bookmark icon. Set body text at 17 pt with Dynamic Type, and let a card scroll inside itself instead of cutting off safety text. If steps within a skill use segments, make taps advance them and fill each segment only when its step is done, never on a timer. Users should be able to swipe past an unanswered quiz without it counting as complete. A wrong answer should always show the right answer and why. A finished skill should drop out of "continue." The "Checked against N sources" line directly answers the trust gap reviewers raised about Scroll The Bible's AI-generated content, so keep it visible.

**Onboarding and paywall.** Use a guest, play-first flow. A short quiz ("7 quick questions to tailor your feed") leads into a Readiness Score framed as "Here's where you're starting," never as a percentage of how unready the user is. Then show at least one real card before a soft paywall with a close button visible right away. Offer $39.99/yr, which sits at the Education median, preselected with a 7-day trial, plus a monthly option. Show a Today / Day 5 reminder / Day 7 timeline, a real reminder before the trial ends, Restore and Manage Subscription links, and plain labels such as "Start 7-day free trial" instead of "Try for $0.00." Bring the paywall back at natural Go Deeper taps and for users returning after Day 7, since most paywall views happen after the first session. Skip commitment rituals, addiction framing, and loss-framed exit modals.

**Habits and navigation.** Count one card as a streak day. Include a free repair or freeze from the start, celebrate days 3 and 7, and make the streak optional. Consider "days learned this week" as the main measure, since preparedness isn't naturally a daily practice like language study. Ask for notification permission after the first save or finished skill. Send one calm notification a day built around content ("Today's card: storing water for two weeks"), rotate the copy, and pull back for lapsed users. A card-of-the-day widget with week dots, plus content planned around hurricane, winter, and planting seasons, matches the verse-of-the-day pattern. Give each of the four tabs one job: Feed scrolls, Learn browses the 9 themes (under Baymard's 10-option limit) with a Continue row, Kit is the checklist, and You holds streaks, saves, and settings. Hide empty tracks behind one quiet "More coming" line. When a card is shared as an image, it must not lose its safety caveats.

**Shipping.** Bundle content so first launch works offline. Every first aid card should carry a "call 911" line, and the description should say the app is educational. Use PostHog without IDFA, so no ATT prompt is needed, but disclose it and offer an opt-out. Host the policy, terms, and support pages before submitting. Write review notes that show the reviewer where the paywall is. Plan for one to two weeks and at least one rejection round.

## Conclusion

The research turns down an assumption that runs through this category: that calm and conversion pull against each other. The two best-documented wins, Blinkist's honest trial reminder and Duolingo's easier, forgiving streak, are both acts of restraint that *raised* revenue and retention. The dark patterns (guilt headlines, commitment pacts, delayed close buttons, stale "continue" nags) bring in short-term trials, and the costs show up in refunds, 1-star reviews, and month-one cancellations. Nearly 30% of annual subscriptions cancel in their first month ([RevenueCat](https://revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2025)). For a safety-content app, trust is also the thing the product sells, so the verification line, the 911 prompts, and an honest paywall all serve the same purpose as the marketing.

The open questions need user testing, not more desk research. Nobody has measured whether segment bars inside a vertical feed confuse people, whether a weekly goal keeps users better than a daily streak, or whether a composite readiness score motivates or worries them. Scroll Ready should treat these as its first experiments. Ship the simplest version of each, as Duolingo advises, and measure against all-category benchmarks of about **25% D1, 8% D7, and 4% D30** ([UXCam](https://uxcam.com/blog/mobile-app-retention-benchmarks)). No education-specific figures were found.
